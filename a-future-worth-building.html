This article by Marc Andreessen of Andreessen Horowitz, a Silicon Valley venture capital firm, eloquently puts into words a vision I have long dreamed of but could never articulate.

I

It’s a society dedicated to technological progress. It doesn’t hold religion, hedonism, or fleeting pleasures in the highest regard. Rather, its members are driven by a devotion to technology, a devouring inner curiosity to explore the scientific workings of the universe, and a determination to reach the ever-receding boundaries of engineering possibility.

People express their patriotism and devotion to this society by applying themselves to the cause of technological advancement.

Why? What would they gain from it?

They gain a sense of purpose in life. Science is at once an intensely humbling and incredibly egotistical experience.

You work toward something that countless people before you have contributed to, bringing you to where you stand today. You are incredibly tiny on this scale.

Science is at once an intensely humbling and incredibly egotistical experience.

Yet the power accumulated over those millennia is immense. And you, standing upon the efforts of countless generations, wield it. Does that power belong to you? Have you earned the right to exercise it? Perhaps not. But drunk on the immediate ecstasy of discovery, you hardly care.

Such a society would offer its citizens something more enduring than hedonistic happiness: a sense of purpose. Its members would devote themselves to a cause greater than the individual, knowing that their work could outlast them by centuries. Those who risk their lives at the frontiers of discovery will be remembered and honored like the patriots of the Grande Armée, their feats of devotion to the goals of humanity admired by all.

II

As Marc Andreessen argues, technology and devotion to its development truly do offer liberation from the shackles that nature has imposed upon humanity, and I agree with every word he writes. His vision is one I wholeheartedly share, and I could hardly imagine a more compelling articulation of it.

Unfortunately, the manifesto is written with an almost bitter tone, as one might expect from someone who has spent much of his life battling for a better world while facing constant attempts to restrain his efforts. I will therefore rewrite parts of it in a more charitable spirit, in an attempt to convince the unbelievers and address what I anticipate will be common rebuttals.

If technological progress is more than a means to prosperity—if it is itself a source of meaning—then the consequences of obstructing it extend beyond delayed inventions or lost economic growth. We deny people not merely the fruits of discovery, but the opportunity to participate in it. To restrain human ingenuity unnecessarily is to diminish one of the very things that makes life worth living.

III

Andreessen writes:

Our present society has been subjected to a mass demoralization campaign for six decades – against technology and against life – under varying names like ‘existential risk’, ‘sustainability’, ‘ESG’, ‘Sustainable Development Goals’, ‘social responsibility’, ‘stakeholder capitalism’, ‘Precautionary Principle’, ‘trust and safety’, ‘tech ethics’, ‘risk management’, ‘de-growth’, ‘the limits of growth’.— Marc Andreessen, The Techno-Optimist Manifesto

If there is any point in the text that exemplifies Andreessen’s frustrations and is most likely to irritate readers, it’s this. And for good reason: who would seek to demonize causes with such noble-sounding names?

It is because these causes are performative, undertaken with little actual purpose other than to assert the ‘goodness’ of their proponents and the evil of their opponents, that Andreessen despises them.

Consider sustainability: for years, one of the greatest targets of the environmental movement wasn’t coal or other highly polluting energy sources, but nuclear power plants, perhaps among the cleanest and, contrary to popular belief, safest sources of energy.

Existential risk—certainly, powerful technologies may carry some dangers. But if the possibility of misuse were sufficient grounds for prohibition, where would we stop? At artificial intelligence? At the linear algebra underlying it? Furthermore, we commit the greatest crime possible by enacting such bans: hindering human and technological discovery.

Perhaps most damning are appeals to social responsibility, stakeholder capitalism, and tech ethics. One of the greatest ironies I’ve perceived is that socialists and capitalists each regard the greatest risk of the other’s system as the financial greed of those in power.

IV

There is certainly a hunger, a drive, in each person, but from what I’ve seen, it’s rarely a desire for money or power as an end in itself. In our current world, some seek money or power to advance their religion, others to support a charity, and still others, as in Marc Andreessen’s case (and my own), simply to build cool things and advance technology. This is hardly an evil goal; we are hardly an evil species. We are social; we have been bred to relish helping, not harming, each other. We therefore ought not to be so suspicious of each other’s goals but should instead help remove roadblocks for others rather than placing more in their way.

A future is waiting, a truly beautiful one. A future in which technology provides not only the means to live, but a reason to strive; in which every discovery builds upon the achievements of countless generations, and every invention becomes a gift to those yet to come.

We will conquer the stars. We will live forever. Perhaps more importantly, we will find a unifying goal.

Why should we clip our wings before we have even left the ground? Let us not surrender our ambitions to fear, but stretch our minds, hopes, dreams, and imaginations—and build a world worthy of them.
